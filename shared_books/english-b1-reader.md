# B1 graded reader: work, health, the city, disagreement

This is an original graded reader written for this app. It sits at B1: past and present mixed, some conditionals, opinion phrases, not many rare idioms. After the four original scenes you will find a short public-domain Aesop fable and a modern paraphrase.

**Licence.** The four scenes and all study notes are original. “The Fox and the Grapes” below follows a public-domain English telling in the Aesop tradition (the fable itself is ancient; this wording is a teaching reconstruction in the open fable style, not a copyrighted modern picture-book).

---

## What this book is

A B1 reader is a bridge. It is easier than a newspaper and harder than A1 dialogues about tea. You should be able to read each scene in five to eight minutes, then say it.

Themes: work, health, city life, disagreement. These are the topics of adult life. They also match later English lessons on advice, complaints, and giving reasons.

---

## Honest analysis: graded vs authentic text

**What graded text does well**

- It controls vocabulary. You meet *shift*, *queue*, *appointment*, *I see your point* more than once.
- It keeps grammar in a corridor: Past Simple, Present Perfect for recent results, first conditional, a little reported speech.
- It lets you finish. Finishing builds the habit that authentic text later needs.

**What graded text cannot do**

- It is slightly too tidy. Real colleagues interrupt. Real doctors hedge. Real arguments loop.
- It under-uses idiom and irony. Holmes (B2 file) and Grimm (German file) will shock you after this. That shock is useful — but only after B1 is stable.
- It can sound “textbook.” We accept that. The job is repetition with a story, not literature.

**How we use it**

Read → highlight three phrases you want in your mouth → cards → say the dialogue with a timer (90 seconds per scene). Then read Aesop: authentic-simple, not graded. Notice the difference in rhythm.

## Study method

1. Read the scene silently.
2. Highlight chunks, not single words (*I’m on the late shift*).
3. Cards: chunk on the front, your language on the back.
4. Say the scene aloud, then close the file and retell in six sentences.
5. For Aesop: read the old-style paragraph, then the paraphrase, then tell the moral in one modern sentence.

## Scene 1 — Work (original)

Marta works in a warehouse on the edge of the city. She starts at six. The building is cold in the morning. Her supervisor, Ken, is usually fair, but this week two people are ill and the line is slow.

Ken: Marta, can you stay until eight? We have a late lorry.

Marta: I can stay until half past seven. I have a class at eight.

Ken: What’s the class?

Marta: English. If I miss it, I wait a whole week.

Ken: That’s fair. Do the last pallet with Jamal, then go. I’ll ask night shift to finish.

Jamal joins her. He has been at the company for three months. He still mixes *shift* and *sheet*.

Jamal: Is this the last one?

Marta: Yes. After this I go. You should drink water. You look grey.

Jamal: I didn’t sleep. My neighbour played music until three. I wanted to knock, but I didn’t.

Marta: Tomorrow, write a note. Or talk in the morning, not at three. If you shout at night, it becomes a war.

They finish. Marta clocks out. On the bus she feels the good tiredness of a finished shift, not the bad tiredness of a fight with Ken. She texts her sister: *Left on time. Class still possible.*

**Chunks:** late lorry, clock out, I can stay until…, If I miss it…, it becomes a war.

**Task:** Retell Ken’s problem in three sentences. Use *have to* once and *if* once.

## Scene 2 — Health (original)

On Thursday Marta has a sore throat. She does not go to the emergency department. She books a morning appointment at the clinic. The waiting room is full of people looking at phones.

Receptionist: Name and date of birth?

Marta: Marta Silva, 4 March 1996.

Nurse: What brings you in today?

Marta: My throat hurts when I swallow. It started on Tuesday. No fever at home. I’m tired.

Nurse: Any cough? Any trouble breathing?

Marta: A dry cough at night. Breathing is fine.

Doctor: I need to look at your throat. Open, please. It is red, not dangerous from what I see. Rest, fluids, and if you get a high fever or you cannot swallow water, come back or call. This is everyday advice in a story, not a diagnosis of *you*.

Marta: Can I still go to work?

Doctor: If you feel able, yes, but don’t share cups. Tell your supervisor you may need a quieter day.

On the street Marta almost buys antibiotics from a friend of a friend. She remembers an English sentence from class: *Antibiotics are not sweets.* She buys honey and a lemon instead. She is not a doctor. She is a reader practising the difference between a story and a prescription.

**Chunks:** What brings you in today?, when I swallow, trouble breathing, come back if…

**Task:** Write five questions a nurse might ask. Keep them B1.

## Scene 3 — City (original)

Saturday. Marta and Jamal meet at the station because the city is doing a night market. The square is loud. A tram is delayed. A man at the kiosk sells tea in paper cups.

Jamal: Where is the market? The map says “behind the old post office,” but I don’t see a post office.

Marta: Let’s ask. Excuse me, we’re looking for the night market.

Woman: Go past the tram stop, then left at the bookshop. You’ll hear it before you see it.

They walk. A cyclist rings a bell. A driver shouts. The city is not polite, but it is readable if you have phrases.

At the market a stall sells second-hand coats. The price card says 40. Jamal says 30. The seller laughs, then says 35. They pay 35. This is not a lesson in “always bargain.” It is a lesson in *Can you do 35?* and hearing *That’s my last price.*

On the way back the tram is packed. A teenager does not give a seat to an older man. Marta feels angry and says nothing. Later she tells her class: *I wanted to speak, but I didn’t have the sentence.* The teacher gives her the sentence: *Excuse me, could he sit here?* Having the sentence does not guarantee courage. It removes one excuse.

**Chunks:** We’re looking for…, You’ll hear it before you see it, That’s my last price, packed.

**Task:** Give directions from a station you know to a market or supermarket, in English, eight sentences.

## Scene 4 — Disagreement (original)

Monday meeting. Ken wants the team to start at 5:30 for one month. Marta’s class is in the evening, but 5:30 would kill her sleep. Jamal hates conflict. He looks at the table.

Ken: It’s only four weeks. We have a contract.

Marta: I understand the contract. I don’t agree that 5:30 is the only answer. If we overlap with night shift for an hour, we still finish the lorries.

Ken: Night shift is already tired.

Marta: Then we hire a temp for the peak. I know it costs money. So does people leaving.

Jamal: I can do two early days, not five. That’s my limit.

Ken: I’ll think. I’m not saying yes.

After the meeting Jamal says Marta was “too hard.” Marta says Jamal was “too soft.” They are both partly right. B1 English must hold two truths: *I see your point* and *I still don’t agree.*

That night Marta practises in the kitchen:

- I see the problem with the lorries.
- I don’t think 5:30 works for me.
- Here’s another option.
- What would you need to say yes?

She does not win a film victory. On Wednesday Ken offers three early days, not five. Marta takes it. Disagreement in adult life often ends in a smaller number, not a speech.

**Chunks:** I see your point, I don’t agree that…, That’s my limit, I’ll think.

**Task:** Disagree with a friend about a meeting time. Use one softening phrase and one clear *no*.

## Public-domain fable: The Fox and the Grapes

A fox was walking through an orchard when he saw a bunch of grapes hanging from a high vine. The grapes looked ripe and dark. He jumped for them. He missed. He jumped again, harder. He still missed. After many tries he walked away and said, as if to the air, “Those grapes are sour. I do not want them.”

(This is the traditional moral shape of Aesop’s fable: we mock what we cannot reach. The story exists in many public-domain English versions; the wording here is a short teaching text in that open tradition.)

### Modern B1 paraphrase

A fox wants some grapes. They are too high. He tries and fails. Then he says the grapes are bad, so that he does not feel stupid. People do this with jobs, cities, and relationships: *I didn’t want it anyway.*

**Why put Aesop next to graded scenes?**

The fable is short, old, and slightly sharp. Graded Scene 4 *explains* disagreement. Aesop *shows* a defence mechanism without a psychologist’s paragraph. Authentic-simple text still needs inference (why does he say sour?). That is the step toward Holmes.

**Speaking:** Tell the fable in the present (*The fox sees… he jumps… he says…*). Then in the past. Then give a modern example from work or the city in four sentences, without preaching.
