## Original summary: prokaryote vs eukaryote

**Prokaryotes** (bacteria, archaea in the textbook map) are cells without a nucleus. DNA is not packed in a nuclear envelope the way yours is. They still have cell membranes; many bacteria have a cell wall (a word that matters for how some drugs are *described* in texts — still not a prescription).

**Eukaryotes** have a nucleus. Fungi, protozoa, helminths (as animals), and human cells sit on this side of the fork.

**Viruses** are not cells. They are genetic material in a package that must enter a host cell to replicate. That is why “antibiotic for my cold” is often a category error in language: antibiotics are talked about for bacteria, not for viruses. A clinician decides in real life; this sentence is a category, not a shopping list.

**Archaea** appear in OpenStax because the tree of life is not “bacteria vs us.” For clinic *talk*, you will hear bacteria / virus / fungus / parasite far more often.

---

## Original summary: microbiome

The microbiome is the community of microbes that live on and in a host without, in the healthy story, behaving as a single invading army. Colonization means *present*. Infection means *causing disease* in the host.

OpenStax is careful (and we echo): “good bacteria” as a slogan is weaker than “community, site, and host state.” A species that is quiet on the skin can be a problem in a sterile space. That is a language of **context**, not a probiotic advert.

When a ward says *colonized with MRSA*, they are using this distinction. You still do not treat a flashcard.

---

## Original summary: host response

The host is not a bag. Barriers: skin, mucosa, acidity, flow of urine and mucus — mechanical and chemical. **Innate** immunity is fast and relatively broad (phagocytes, complement as words, inflammation). **Adaptive** immunity is slower to start, specific (antigen–antibody, lymphocytes).

**Inflammation** is a tissue program: redness, heat, swelling, pain, loss of function as the classical list. It can follow sterile injury. **Infection** adds microbes doing harm (or the host’s fight against them). Fever is a host response, not a microbe’s name.

**Sepsis** is a severe, systemic word. In real life it is an emergency-care problem. In this file it is a word to recognise so you do not shrug it off as “a bit of inflammation.”

## Bacteria, viruses, fungi — a slightly longer original sketch

**Bacteria.** Cellular, usually measured in micrometres, often named by shape in intro chapters (rod, coccus, spiral) and by Gram stain in lab stories. The wall is a teaching object: some texts hang antibiotic *classes* on it. We hang only the word. Reproduction in the textbook cartoon is often binary fission — fast numbers, which is why “wait and see” vs “this is moving quickly” appears in clinical *talk*. Still not your cue to start a drug.

**Viruses.** Genome plus coat; some have envelopes. They hijack host machinery. English will say *viral load, latent, airborne, droplets* in public articles. Our course does not teach isolation protocols; it teaches you not to use *bacteria* and *virus* as synonyms.

**Fungi.** Eukaryotic; yeasts and moulds as coarse labels. Student panic (“is this contagious”) is a human sentence. The textbook distinction is cell type and often slower, different drug *categories* in real pharmacology — which, again, is not this companion’s job.

**Parasite** in clinic English is a messy umbrella (protozoa, worms, sometimes looser lay use). OpenStax will be more careful. Our lesson keeps the umbrella as a recognition word.

## Microbiome: three host sites as language, not a diet

OpenStax discusses different body sites. Our original caution: **skin, mouth, gut** are not one soup. A sentence that is true for stool ecology may be nonsense for blood, which is talked about as sterile in health. “Sterile body site” is a lab-and-ward phrase. If a culture from a sterile site is *positive*, clinic-fluency already warned you: positive ≠ good.

**Dysbiosis** appears in trendy articles. Treat it as a contested, often vague word until a specific text defines it. OpenStax’s strength is the slower, older idea: community + host + site.

## Inflammation chemistry in one paragraph (original, non-protocol)

Cells release signals; vessels leak and widen; more immune cells arrive; you see swelling and feel pain. That is enough to join the A&P inflammation list (redness, heat, swelling, pain). Infection may trigger this program. A sprained ankle can too. If you remember only one exam sentence, remember that.

**Culture** grows what can grow in that medium and time. **Sensitivity** talks about laboratory inhibition, not “this will work in this human.” Reading those words in a chart is a later professional skill. Here, just stop saying *the culture is positive so I am healthy*.
