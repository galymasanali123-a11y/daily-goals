# A Scandal in Bohemia — B2+ study companion

This file is a study companion, not a complete novel. It is for learners in this app who already manage B1 grammar and want denser Victorian narrative English.

## What this book is

You will read the opening of the first Holmes short story: Watson’s portrait of Holmes, a gap of months, a night on Baker Street, and the first deductions about marriage and practice. Then you will study why the prose is useful at B2+, not merely “famous.”

Holmes is not a graded reader. Sentences stack clauses. Dialogue hides information. The narrator (Watson) is biased. That combination is exactly what B2+ reading exams and university humanities courses expect you to do: hold a long sentence, hear a voice, and infer what is not said.

---

## Honest analysis of the source

**Strengths for language study**

- **Syntax density.** Doyle likes coordinated and subordinated clauses in one breath. If you can parse “He was, I take it, the most perfect reasoning and observing machine that the world has seen,” you can parse a modern news lead.
- **Dialogue as data.** Holmes does not small-talk. Every remark is a claim. You practise listening for implication (“Wedlock suits you” = I have already measured you).
- **Stable narrator.** Watson is warm, slightly pompous, and often one step behind. That gap between what Watson reports and what Holmes knows is inference training.
- **Period flavour with modern descendants.** Formal 19th-century English still sits under legal, academic, and some clinical registers. You will meet *eclipses*, *predominates*, *abhorrent*, *gibe*.

**Limits**

- **Dated social world.** Gender, class, and the treatment of Irene Adler as “*the* woman” are of 1891. We read them as history of language, not as a model of how to speak about people now.
- **Drug references.** Holmes’s cocaine is part of the canon and of the public-domain text. It is not an invitation and not a medical discussion.
- **Vocabulary load.** Some words (*gasogene*, *woo*, *harness* in the sense of returning to work) need glosses. Do not pretend you “should already know them.”
- **We do not use Holmes as a detective course.** The skill here is English, not criminology.

**How we use it in the app**

Read the excerpt once for plot. Mark. Make cards from the 20-word list. Say the dialogue aloud. Then do the three speaking tasks. The English B2 lessons in the course train narrative tenses and inversion in smaller bites; this file is the authentic stretch.

## Study method

1. **Read** the excerpt once without a dictionary. Underline names and time words.
2. **Highlight** three long sentences. Split each into main clause + extras on paper.
3. **Cards** — front: English word from the list; back: your language + the Doyle example.
4. **Say out loud** Watson’s first paragraph and the “Wedlock suits you” exchange. Record yourself once.
5. **Only then** check the modern B2 equivalents. If you modernise too early, you never feel the 19th-century rhythm.

## Core content: public-domain excerpt

From “A Scandal in Bohemia,” Part I (Project Gutenberg, *The Adventures of Sherlock Holmes*).

To Sherlock Holmes she is always *the* woman. I have seldom heard him mention her under any other name. In his eyes she eclipses and predominates the whole of her sex. It was not that he felt any emotion akin to love for Irene Adler. All emotions, and that one particularly, were abhorrent to his cold, precise but admirably balanced mind. He was, I take it, the most perfect reasoning and observing machine that the world has seen, but as a lover he would have placed himself in a false position. He never spoke of the softer passions, save with a gibe and a sneer. They were admirable things for the observer—excellent for drawing the veil from men’s motives and actions. But for the trained reasoner to admit such intrusions into his own delicate and finely adjusted temperament was to introduce a distracting factor which might throw a doubt upon all his mental results. Grit in a sensitive instrument, or a crack in one of his own high-power lenses, would not be more disturbing than a strong emotion in a nature such as his. And yet there was but one woman to him, and that woman was the late Irene Adler, of dubious and questionable memory.

I had seen little of Holmes lately. My marriage had drifted us away from each other. My own complete happiness, and the home-centred interests which rise up around the man who first finds himself master of his own establishment, were sufficient to absorb all my attention, while Holmes, who loathed every form of society with his whole Bohemian soul, remained in our lodgings in Baker Street, buried among his old books, and alternating from week to week between cocaine and ambition, the drowsiness of the drug, and the fierce energy of his own keen nature. He was still, as ever, deeply attracted by the study of crime, and occupied his immense faculties and extraordinary powers of observation in following out those clues, and clearing up those mysteries which had been abandoned as hopeless by the official police. From time to time I heard some vague account of his doings: of his summons to Odessa in the case of the Trepoff murder, of his clearing up of the singular tragedy of the Atkinson brothers at Trincomalee, and finally of the mission which he had accomplished so delicately and successfully for the reigning family of Holland. Beyond these signs of his activity, however, which I merely shared with all the readers of the daily press, I knew little of my former friend and companion.

One night—it was on the twentieth of March, 1888—I was returning from a journey to a patient (for I had now returned to civil practice), when my way led me through Baker Street. As I passed the well-remembered door, which must always be associated in my mind with my wooing, and with the dark incidents of the Study in Scarlet, I was seized with a keen desire to see Holmes again, and to know how he was employing his extraordinary powers. His rooms were brilliantly lit, and, even as I looked up, I saw his tall, spare figure pass twice in a dark silhouette against the blind. He was pacing the room swiftly, eagerly, with his head sunk upon his chest and his hands clasped behind him. To me, who knew his every mood and habit, his attitude and manner told their own story. He was at work again. He had risen out of his drug-created dreams and was hot upon the scent of some new problem. I rang the bell and was shown up to the chamber which had formerly been in part my own.

His manner was not effusive. It seldom was; but he was glad, I think, to see me. With hardly a word spoken, but with a kindly eye, he waved me to an armchair, threw across his case of cigars, and indicated a spirit case and a gasogene in the corner. Then he stood before the fire and looked me over in his singular introspective fashion.

“Wedlock suits you,” he remarked. “I think, Watson, that you have put on seven and a half pounds since I saw you.”

“Seven!” I answered.

“Indeed, I should have thought a little more. Just a trifle more, I fancy, Watson. And in practice again, I observe. You did not tell me that you intended to go into harness.”

End of the excerpt used in this companion. The rest of the story is also public domain if you continue on Gutenberg.

## Vocabulary notes (not a full glossary)

**eclipses / predominates** — she outshines and dominates, in Holmes’s ranking. Modern: *she outshines everyone else, in his view*.

**abhorrent** — disgusting, unacceptable to him. Stronger than *he doesn’t like*.

**gibe and a sneer** — a mocking remark and a contemptuous look. Register: literary.

**drawing the veil from** — revealing what is hidden. Metaphor from covering a face or a statue.

**grit in a sensitive instrument** — sand in a precision machine. Extended metaphor for emotion as contamination of reason.

**lodgings** — rented rooms. Modern: *flat* / *apartment* (Baker Street is still “rooms” in fan talk).

**civil practice** — Watson is a doctor again, not in the army. *Practice* = medical work, not “rehearsal.”

**wooing** — courtship. Old-fashioned; modern *when I was falling in love / dating*.

**spare figure** — thin, not extra flesh.

**blind** — window covering. British; US often *shade*.

**effusive** — pouring out feeling. Holmes is not.

**wedlock** — marriage (legal/poetic). Modern: *marriage*.

**go into harness** — return to regular work, like a horse put in a harness. Idiom, not veterinary advice.

**gasogene** — a Victorian soda-water gadget. You need the gloss; you will not need the object.

## Grammar: narrative tenses, inversion, then and now

### Narrative tenses in the excerpt

- **Past Simple** for the night’s actions: *I passed*, *I saw*, *I rang*.
- **Past Perfect** for the gap before that night: *I had seen little*, *My marriage had drifted us away*, *He had risen out of his drug-created dreams*.
- **Past Continuous** for the picture in the window: *He was pacing*.
- **Present** for the timeless portrait: *To Sherlock Holmes she is always the woman.* Watson opens in a general present, then drops into memoir.

B2 task: mark every verb in the silhouette paragraph. You should see a film (continuous) interrupted by a conclusion in Simple: *He was at work again.*

### Inversion and fronting (light, not a full grammar chapter)

Doyle fronts time and circumstance:

- *One night—it was on the twentieth of March, 1888—I was returning…*
- *With hardly a word spoken… he waved me to an armchair.*

This is not full literary inversion like *Never had I seen*, but it is the same family: put the scene-setting chunk first. At B2 you already know *Never have I…* for emphasis. Here, practise *With hardly a word spoken, he…* as a spoken summary.

*Seldom* with inversion appears in related Holmes prose (*seldom have I*). In this excerpt you have *I have seldom heard him* — adverb after the auxiliary, not inverted. Compare:

- Doyle-like: *I have seldom heard him mention her.*
- Emphatic inversion (exam favourite): *Seldom have I heard him mention her.*

Know both. Speak the first; recognise the second.

### Formal 19th-c vs modern B2 equivalents

| Doyle | Modern B2 |
| --- | --- |
| she eclipses and predominates the whole of her sex | she outshines every other woman, in his mind |
| abhorrent to his cold, precise mind | he found emotions, especially love, unacceptable |
| master of his own establishment | he had his own home |
| I was seized with a keen desire | I suddenly really wanted |
| Wedlock suits you | Married life looks good on you |
| go into harness | go back to work as a doctor |
| a trifle more | a little more |

Do not “correct” Doyle when you read. Translate down only when you *speak* the summary.

### Inference (why Holmes is good B2+ reading)

Watson never writes “Holmes had a new case.” He gives: bright lamps, pacing, head down, hands behind back. You infer. Exam papers call this *reading between the lines*. It is also what you do with a patient’s story: the words plus the picture.

A second inference layer: Holmes weighs Watson without asking. Weight, return to practice — observed, then stated. The comedy is Watson’s protest (*Seven!*). The language lesson is: English can accuse politely.

## Three speaking tasks

**Task 1 — 60-second portrait (narrative tenses).** Tell a partner who Holmes is, using at least one Present for the general fact and two Past Perfect forms for the time apart. Do not read. If you stall, start: *To Holmes, Irene Adler is still the woman. I had not seen him…*

**Task 2 — Modernise the reunion.** Replay the armchair scene in 2020s English, same facts (weight, work, cigars optional). Keep it B2, not slang soup. Then say which version sounds ruder. (Often the modern one, because Doyle’s formality cushions the insult.)

**Task 3 — Inference aloud.** Describe only what Watson *sees* in the street (door, light, silhouette, pacing). Your partner must infer *he is working*. Swap.
