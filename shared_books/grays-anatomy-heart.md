# Gray’s Anatomy (1918) — the heart as a hollow organ

A short public-domain excerpt on the heart, then a modern English restatement, vocabulary, and a warning about date.

**Source.** Henry Gray, *Anatomy of the Human Body*, 20th edition (1918), the Bartleby / Gutenberg tradition of the American 20th edition. The wording below is the well-known 20th-edition public-domain text on the heart and, briefly, the pericardium. **Public domain** in the United States.

## What this book is

Gray 1918 is a descriptive atlas in prose. It tells you *where* and *what shape*. It does not tell you 2020s treatment. We read it for terminology and spatial English.

---

## Honest analysis of the source

**Strengths**

- **Spatial description.** Oblique in the chest, one-third right / two-thirds left of the midline, conical, enclosed in pericardium — these sentences train prepositions (*behind, between, toward*).
- **Stable names.** Atrium, ventricle, pericardium, myocardium, endocardium, sulcus — the nouns survived. You will meet them in OpenStax and in clinic letters.
- **Public domain.** You may quote a short excerpt in class without a permissions maze.
- **Chambers logic.** Septa divide right/left; each half has atrium above, ventricle below. That paragraph is still the right cartoon.

**Limits — do not use 1918 as treatment**

- **Physiology and disease care are outdated or missing.** No modern ACS pathway, no echocardiography language as used today, no evidence-based drug talk.
- **“Venous / impure” vs “arterial / pure.”** Gray’s introduction still talks that way. We translate to oxygen-poor / oxygen-rich, and we define artery by *direction*.
- **Measurements.** Adult size and weight ranges are 1918 textbook figures, not your personal targets.
- **Dissection tone.** Ligature of arteries, etc., belongs to a surgical era’s teaching, not to an app protocol.
- **Language.** *Auricle* in older use sometimes names what we now carefully split (atrium vs auricular appendage). Check modern labels when you speak.

**How we use it**

One excerpt for the hollow organ, chambers, and pericardium idea. Then modern restatement. Then stop. OpenStax for cycle and vessels; clinic lessons for *chest pain* words.

---

## Study method

Read Gray once aloud, slowly. Mark every preposition of place. Rewrite the same facts in 2020s English without looking. Check against our translation. Cards for ten nouns. Do not “follow Gray” if someone is ill.

## Public-domain excerpt (20th ed., 1918)

From V. Angiology, 4b. The Heart (Cor), and the introduction / pericardium discussion in the same edition.

The heart is a hollow muscular organ of a somewhat conical form; it lies between the lungs in the middle mediastinum and is enclosed in the pericardium. It is placed obliquely in the chest behind the body of the sternum and adjoining parts of the rib cartilages, and projects farther into the left than into the right half of the thoracic cavity, so that about one-third of it is situated on the right and two-thirds on the left of the median plane.

Component Parts.—The heart is subdivided by septa into right and left halves, and a constriction subdivides each half of the organ into two cavities, the upper cavity being called the atrium, the lower the ventricle. The heart therefore consists of four chambers, viz., right and left atria, and right and left ventricles.

The heart is the central organ of the blood vascular system, and consists of a hollow muscle; by its contraction the blood is pumped to all parts of the body through a complicated series of tubes, termed arteries. The arteries undergo enormous ramification in their course throughout the body, and end in minute vessels, called arterioles, which in their turn open into a close-meshed network of microscopic vessels, termed capillaries. After the blood has passed through the capillaries it is collected into a series of larger vessels, called veins, by which it is returned to the heart.

The human heart is divided by septa into right and left halves, and each half is further divided into two cavities, an upper termed the atrium and a lower the ventricle. The atria are receiving chambers, and the ventricles distributing ones.

The pericardium is a conical fibro-serous sac, in which the heart and the roots of the great vessels are contained. Although the pericardium is usually described as a single sac, an examination of its structure shows that it consists essentially of two sacs intimately connected with one another, but totally different in structure. The outer sac, known as the fibrous pericardium, consists of fibrous tissue. The inner sac, or serous pericardium, is a delicate membrane which lies within the fibrous sac and lines its walls. The serous pericardium consists of a visceral and a parietal portion. The visceral portion, or epicardium, covers the heart.

Structure.—The heart consists of muscular fibers, and of fibrous rings which serve for their attachment. It is covered by the visceral layer of the serous pericardium (epicardium), and lined by the endocardium. Between these two membranes is the muscular wall or myocardium.

(End of excerpt. Figures in the 1918 book are not reproduced here.)

## Modern translation into current clinical English

The heart is a hollow muscle, roughly cone-shaped. It sits between the lungs in the middle mediastinum, inside the pericardium. It does not sit straight on: it is tilted, behind the sternum and nearby rib cartilages, with more of its bulk on the left of the midline than on the right (classically about one-third right, two-thirds left).

A wall (septum) separates a right pump from a left pump. Each side has an **atrium** (upper collecting chamber) and a **ventricle** (lower pumping chamber). Four chambers total.

When the muscle contracts, it drives blood into **arteries** (vessels leaving the heart). Arteries branch to arterioles, then to **capillaries** (exchange). **Veins** bring blood back.

**Atria receive; ventricles distribute.** (Still a good teaching sentence.)

The **pericardium** is a cone-shaped sac around the heart and the roots of the great vessels. Think two layers conceptually: a tough outer **fibrous** sac and an inner **serous** sac. The serous layer that sits on the heart is **epicardium**. The lining inside the chambers is **endocardium**. The muscle between is **myocardium**.

**Update the gas language:** do not say “impure blood” in 2026 notes. Say oxygen-poor or oxygen-rich, and remember pulmonary arteries are still *arteries*.

**Do not** take 1918 sizes or surgical asides as your care plan.

## Why it is still strong for terminology and space

Because English anatomy is a pile of Latin-shaped nouns plus prepositions. Gray writes *between, behind, into, of the median plane*. Learners who only flash *heart = насос* never practise *obliquely in the chest*. Clinic letters still assume you can hear *pericardium* and *mediastinum*.

Because names of chambers did not reset in 1918–2020. The **care** around those chambers did.

## Spatial English drill (original)

Gray’s value is prepositions. Copy these stems, then close the book and say them while pointing at your own chest (you are not dissecting; you are parking vocabulary).

- The heart lies **between** the lungs.  
- It is **enclosed in** the pericardium.  
- It is placed **obliquely** **behind** the sternum.  
- It projects farther **into the left**.  
- About one-third **on the right** of the median plane.  
- Atria are **receiving** chambers; ventricles are **distributing** ones.  
- Epicardium **covers**; endocardium **lines**; myocardium **is between**.

**Contrast with 2020s notes**

| 1918 flavour | Current notes |
| --- | --- |
| impure / pure | oxygen-poor / oxygen-rich |
| auricle (loose) | atrium vs auricle/appendage |
| ramification of arteries | branching |
| viz. | namely / i.e. |

**Apex language (from the same edition, restated).** The apex points down, forward, and to the left; in living people we talk about the PMI (point of maximal impulse) as a *clinical* word. Gray’s centimetres from the midline are 1918 textbook geometry. Do not tattoo them on a patient. Do learn *apex, base, sternocostal, diaphragmatic* as surface words if you continue anatomy English.

## Circulation in Gray’s introduction, modernised

Systemic circuit: left ventricle → body → right atrium.  
Pulmonary circuit: right ventricle → lungs → left atrium.  
Portal exception: many gut veins → liver → hepatic veins → inferior vena cava → right atrium.

That last sentence is why “veins go to the heart” needs a footnote in the abdomen. Our digestive lesson names the portal vein; Gray already knew the two-capillary story.
